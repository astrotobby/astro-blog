The latest MacBook Pro introduces a long-awaited touchscreen, but according to tech reviewer Marques Brownlee, this feature is far from the device's most compelling advancement. Instead, Brownlee asserts that the revolutionary ProLED XDR display and a surprisingly lighter, thinner chassis are the true game-changers for Apple's professional laptop lineup. While Apple's marketing prominently features users interacting with the touch display, Brownlee's extensive testing suggests it's a secondary, almost negligible, aspect of the user experience.

## A Display Beyond Compare

Brownlee unequivocally identifies the new tandem OLED display as the primary reason to consider the latest MacBook Pro. He describes it as the best display he has ever encountered in a laptop, surpassing even other high-end OLED and mini-LED panels. The specifications alone are impressive, boasting 1000 nits of sustained full-screen brightness and a peak of 2000 nits, ensuring excellent visibility even in bright daylight.

However, the real impact comes from the display's astounding 10 million-to-1 contrast ratio. OLED technology naturally excels with deep blacks and vibrant contrast, but Brownlee notes this particular implementation is exceptionally well-tuned for color accuracy, offering complete coverage of Adobe RGB and P3 color spaces in a single mode. Unlike mini-LED screens, OLED panels feature individually lit pixels, eliminating any possibility of blooming. Additionally, the OLED's faster pixel response time enhances motion fluidity, and improved off-axis viewing angles contribute to its superior visual experience. Minor design tweaks, like a slightly larger screen area due to thinner bezels and a switch from a notch to a Dynamic Island, further refine the display's appeal. Brownlee believes that for professionals heavily reliant on color accuracy in fields like photography or videography, the display alone justifies the price difference compared to previous mini-LED models.

## Navigating the Nano-Texture Dilemma

The decision to opt for a nano-texture finish on the new OLED display presents a more complex choice, according to Brownlee. Traditionally, nano-texture has been a clear advantage for users working in varied lighting conditions, effectively reducing reflections from direct light sources. However, with the new ProLED XDR display, the trade-offs are more pronounced. Brownlee observed that the glossy version of the OLED screen already manages ambient light reflections remarkably well.

The main consideration for nano-texture now involves its subtle impact on the OLED’s inherent strengths. While it still excels at diffusing point light sources, nano-texture slightly diminishes the contrast and sharpness that define the OLED experience, a factor that was less critical with mini-LEDs. An interesting side effect Brownlee discovered is that the nano-texture surface is actually smoother for touchscreen gestures, offering less drag than the glossy finish. Conversely, it is also more challenging to clean, as finger oils can become trapped in its microscopic texture. Brownlee advises potential buyers to carefully weigh these factors, ensuring the benefits of nano-texture truly align with their needs before selecting it.

## Thinner, Lighter, and Just as Potent

Beyond the display, Brownlee highlights the new MacBook Pro's redesigned chassis as the second significant improvement. The 14-inch model is now half a pound lighter, and the 16-inch version sheds 0.7 pounds, making a noticeable difference in portability. This reduction in weight and thickness is partly attributed to the OLED display’s lack of a backlight and a completely re-engineered cooling system.

Historically, thinner laptops often compromise thermal performance. Brownlee conducted extensive side-by-side benchmarks between the new ProLED model and an identical M5 Max predecessor. In burst workloads, performance was identical. Critically, even during extended tasks like video editing and 3D rendering over 10 to 30 minutes, both machines maintained neck-and-neck performance. The new design achieves this by spinning the fans harder and redirecting hot air more efficiently out the back and sides, rather than towards the display. Brownlee expresses satisfaction that the new, sleeker design maintains the robust thermal performance users expect from a MacBook Pro. For him, the lighter 16-inch model makes it a much more viable option for daily carry, and the 14-inch now weighs even less than the 15-inch MacBook Air, enhancing its appeal as a compact powerhouse.

## The Little Things That Add Up

Brownlee notes several "little things" that collectively enhance the user experience, forming the third key reason to consider this new MacBook Pro. A nostalgic favorite is the return of the backlit Apple logo on the laptop's lid, which lights up in sync with the display brightness and can be disabled via a terminal command. The speakers have also received an upgrade, now tuned for richer bass and offering dedicated optimization for use in clamshell mode when docked. For those requiring massive storage, a new 16TB SSD option is available. While battery life is officially rated as identical to mini-LED models, the OLED technology allows for slightly better endurance in dark mode. Finally, the webcam now features increased stabilization, a practical addition given the potential for screen interaction during video calls.

## The Touchscreen: A Checked Box, Not a Revolution

Despite the prominent marketing, Brownlee characterizes the inclusion of a touchscreen as merely "a box checked" by Apple. He explains that it doesn't represent a radical shift in the MacBook Pro's identity or functionality. Apple has not redesigned the hinge to allow the screen to fold flat for easier touch interaction, nor has it introduced Apple Pencil support. The hinge is only slightly stiffer to reduce wobble.

Mac OS adaptations for touch are minimal, with minor changes like two-tap "stoplight" buttons, repositioned Spotlight and Siri icons for top-down swiping, and gesture-based window snapping. Brownlee found himself rarely compelled to use the touchscreen, noting that it often leads to tired shoulders, a wobbling display, and fingerprint smudges. While acknowledging it’s the most responsive laptop touchscreen he's ever used, potentially useful for iOS developers testing apps or for iPhone mirroring, he concludes that for most users, it’s a feature that can easily be ignored or even disabled.

**Key Takeaways:**
*   The ProLED XDR display is the most significant upgrade, offering best-in-class visuals and contrast.
*   The new MacBook Pro is significantly lighter and thinner while maintaining identical thermal performance.
*   Minor enhancements like the backlit Apple logo, improved speakers, and increased storage options add value.
*   The touchscreen, despite heavy marketing, is largely an incidental feature for most users, with minimal macOS integration and no major design changes to facilitate its use.

## Why It Matters

Marques Brownlee suggests that Apple's strategy with the new MacBook Pro, particularly the addition of the touchscreen and other "little things," is to create compelling reasons for existing users to upgrade. With Apple Silicon chips like the M1, M2, and M3 already offering exceptional performance, raw speed improvements in newer M4 or M5 chips might not be enough to drive significant upgrade cycles. Features like the glowing Apple logo, Dynamic Island, and especially the touchscreen, serve as novelties designed to pique interest and differentiate the new models from their still highly capable predecessors. Brownlee indicates that the display and lighter design are compelling enough to make him consider the upgrade, seeing the $400 price difference as justified for the visual fidelity alone. The review ultimately frames the new MacBook Pro as a refinement of an already excellent product line, with the display being the standout innovation, rather than the much-hyped touchscreen.
